Tupper Guardian: a family fridge that remembers for you, built on Step Functions human-in-the-loop

A Telegram bot that tracks leftovers and groceries, asks what happened to each one when it expires, and never lets food disappear from the list just because nobody answered. 100% serverless, no pipeline.

The problem

A slice of cheese nobody remembered

One morning, while I was still wondering what to build for this hackathon, my sister and my mother were at the open fridge, talking about a slice of cheese. It was wrapped in one of those plain plastic bags that local shops in Mexico hand you at the counter. It had what looked like mold, and it smelled bad. Nobody could say how long it had been there, or even when any of us had last eaten from it.

Then it hit me: we had had this exact conversation before. Another food, another container, a can whose date had rubbed off, a bag that never had a date at all.

Food without a date

In Mexico, a lot of food is bought a granel, by weight: ham, cheese, cream, chiles, sliced or scooped at the counter, weighed, and handed over in a plastic bag. There is no sealed package and no printed expiry date. The rule most families follow is simple: if it doesn't smell bad, or even if it doesn't taste bad, it's still good.

Food-safety content on social media is slowly changing that. But many people still believe that cutting the bad part off a tomato makes it fine, or that ham bought by weight fifteen days ago is still safe. My parents grew up with little to spare. For them, throwing food away can feel worse than eating something nobody remembers cooking or buying.

It's about health, not savings

That is the idea behind Tupper Guardian: help families realize that food has a shelf life, often shorter than they think, and that keeping track of it is less about saving money and more about taking care of the people you live with.

And it has to be effortless. Inventory apps already exist, and they fail for the same reason every time: nobody wants to sign up on a website, install a new app, or fill in a form after coming home from the market. So Tupper Guardian lives where families already talk every day, in a chat. In Mexico that means WhatsApp; this prototype uses Telegram because it is quicker to build on.

The solution

Tupper Guardian lives in a chat app people already use (Telegram for this prototype). There is nothing to learn:

  • Tell it or show it. "Guardé caldo de pollo en el refri" (I put chicken soup in the fridge), or a photo of the container. A multimodal model on Amazon Bedrock recognizes the food.
  • Send the shopping list. A typed list or a photo of the receipt becomes one tracked item per food.
  • Food outside the fridge counts too. For a can or a bag of rice the bot asks for the printed expiry date. You can type it or send a photo of the label.
  • It reminds you the day before something expires (with buttons, in case it's already gone), then asks what happened with three buttons: "I ate it", "I threw it away", "It's still there".
  • Nothing disappears by silence. If nobody answers, the food stays on the list flagged for review and the rest of the family is told. A son or daughter can help their parents check the fridge from their own phone.
  • Health first. If someone eats food after telling the bot it was still there past its date, or after it was left for review, the bot doesn't celebrate. It gently explains that eating it can be harmful even if it looks fine.
  • Voice. Confirmations and alerts also arrive as a voice note (Amazon Polly, Mexican Spanish), which helps older users.
The AI understands the message; the expiry date comes from food-safety data. The model never decides how long food is safe.

Architecture

One AWS Step Functions Standard workflow is the backbone. Telegram's webhook calls Amazon API Gateway, which starts an execution directly, with no Lambda in between. Three small AWS Lambda functions cover only what has no native integration: understanding a message, reading and writing the fridge, and sending messages.

ServiceWhy it is there
Amazon API Gateway (REST)Public webhook for Telegram. It starts the workflow itself through a direct StartExecution integration and answers 200 at once. The execution is named tg-<update_id>, so if Telegram delivers an update twice it still runs only once, without any deduplication code. A secret path segment and throttling protect it.
AWS Step Functions (Standard)The heart of the app: it waits for days at no compute cost, re-reads state after every wait, pauses on a task token until a human answers, and fans out one execution per food with a Map state.
AWS Lambda (Python 3.13, arm64)Three small functions: understand, inventory and notify. Each does one job the workflow can't do natively.
Amazon Bedrock (Amazon Nova Lite)Turns free text or a photo into {intent, food, category}, splits a shopping list, and reads printed expiry dates. Its output is validated against a closed list of categories.
Amazon DynamoDB (on-demand)Source of truth. A Tuppers table is partitioned by fridge, so a family shares one partition. A ShelfLife table holds conservative refrigerator times based on USDA FoodKeeper.
Amazon PollyMexican Spanish voice (neural engine, standard as a fallback) for confirmations and alerts, sent as a Telegram voice note.
AWS Systems Manager Parameter StoreHolds the bot token as a SecureString. It never appears in code or in the template.

There are no servers, containers or deployment pipelines. A single script packages the template, deploys it with AWS CloudFormation, seeds the shelf-life table and registers the webhook. When nobody uses the app, nothing runs: every service is pay-per-request.

How one food lives and ends

Every stored food gets its own long-lived execution. This is where Step Functions earns its place over a simple scheduler. The execution waits, re-checks reality, asks a human, waits for the answer, and decides what happens next.

Why not just EventBridge Scheduler? A scheduler can fire a reminder at a time. It can't wait for a person's answer, re-ask tomorrow, take turns with other questions, or escalate to the family when nobody replies. Here all of that is one visible, durable workflow per food, and paused waits cost no compute.

Human-in-the-loop without polling

The question uses lambda:invoke.waitForTaskToken. A Telegram button can carry only 64 bytes, so the token doesn't travel in the button. The notify function saves it on the food's DynamoDB item before sending the buttons, so a fast tap can never arrive ahead of the token it needs.

When someone taps a button, Telegram calls the webhook again and a second, short execution starts. It closes the item with a conditional write (only if it is still ACTIVE) and calls SendTaskSuccess as a direct SDK integration. The paused execution then wakes up. A button, a typed "I already ate it" and the timeout can all race, and exactly one of them wins.

One question at a time, and nothing disappears by silence

The first live test showed a real problem. Three photos stored within a minute expired together, three questions arrived at once, they got lost in the chat, and all three timed out.

Now each person has a single question turn: a slot on their profile taken with a DynamoDB conditional write. It expires by itself, so a failed execution can never block the queue. Executions that find the turn busy wait on a second task token. Whoever answers wakes the next one, and a polling fallback covers the rest.

An unanswered food is no longer deleted. It stays in the fridge flagged for review, the list shows it first, and any member of the family can resolve it.

Shopping lists and pantry food

A shopping list fans out. Bedrock splits it into foods and the inventory function stores them all. A Map state then starts one child execution per food with the Step Functions SDK integration, named tg-<update_id>-<food id>, so a retry can never track the same food twice. Each child joins exactly the same lifecycle as a food stored on its own.

Food that needs no refrigeration waits for its printed expiry date. The bot asks for it one item at a time, and the answer can be typed or be a photo of the label.

Design decisions I'd defend

  • The model extracts, it never decides safety. Bedrock returns an intent, a food name and a category from a closed list. Anything outside that list becomes "other", and any malformed reply becomes "I didn't understand". Shelf life comes from data, not from the model.
  • DynamoDB is the source of truth, Step Functions is the clock. After every Wait the execution re-reads the item with a direct GetItem, so food that was already eaten never triggers an alert.
  • Every close is conditional. Moving an item out of ACTIVE is a conditional update, so a button, a text message and a timeout can race safely.
  • Native integrations over glue code. API Gateway starts executions, and Step Functions reads DynamoDB, releases task tokens and starts child executions itself. Lambda is only used for language, data rules and messaging.
  • Least privilege. Each function and the state machine get only the actions they use. The invite link uses a random single-use code, not a chat id, so a guessed id can't add a stranger to a family's fridge.
  • Demo mode, said out loud. One parameter sets the length of a "day" (86,400 s in real use, about 20 s in the video), so a whole week can be shown in a few minutes.

How I made sure it works

  • 181 automated tests (pytest, with DynamoDB mocked by moto and fake Bedrock and Telegram clients) cover every rule: dates, races between a button and a text message, the question turn, the shared fridge, shopping lists, retries and pantry dates. From the second day on, every rule got a failing test before its code.
  • The state machine is checked against AWS before each deploy: ValidateStateMachineDefinition for the whole definition and TestState for single states with tricky JSONata, such as a child execution's name and input, or a message with no date.
  • Structural tests on the workflow itself: every transition points to a real state, every state is reachable and can finish, every variable is assigned before it is read, and the status is re-read after every Wait.
  • Live runs in demo mode on Telegram and AWS, with the executions inspected in the console. The first round surfaced the "questions arrive all at once" problem that led to the question turn.
  • Built to fail safely: retries on transient Lambda errors, idempotent execution names, conditional writes, a question turn that expires on its own, and an error message to the user instead of silence.

Reuse the pattern

Strip out the food and what remains is a general pattern: remind, ask a human, wait for the answer, escalate to someone else, and never lose the item. It fits anything with a deadline and a person who might not answer:

  • medication refills for an older relative;
  • documents, insurance or warranties about to expire;
  • equipment maintenance in a small business;
  • a daily "are you OK?" check-in that alerts the family when nobody replies.

To reuse it, swap the ShelfLife table for your own durations and rewrite the messages. The workflow, the task-token question, the one-question-at-a-time turn and the family escalation stay the same. Deploying is one command, ./scripts/deploy.sh, with only the AWS CLI and Python 3. Two parameters, SECONDS_PER_DAY and ASK_TIMEOUT_SECONDS, make the timings short enough to demo.

What I learned

  • Real users break your happy path. Testing with real people changed the product: questions now come one at a time, unanswered food stays on the list, and the bot became a family food manager instead of a "rescued containers" counter.
  • Task tokens are the killer feature for human-in-the-loop. A paused Standard execution costs nothing while it waits for a person, and every state transition is visible in the console. That is the part a plain scheduler can't do.
  • JSONata makes the state machine readable. Variables (Assign) and JSONata expressions removed most of the data-shaping Lambdas I expected to write. TestState let me validate single states against AWS before deploying.
  • Small things matter for older users: a voice note, buttons instead of typing, and a message that respects the habit of not wasting food while still putting health first.

What's next

  • WhatsApp as the channel, since it is what most families in Mexico already use.
  • Voice messages as input with Amazon Transcribe.
  • Opened vs. unopened packages, to shorten the date once a package is opened.

Shelf-life values are conservative lower bounds of general USDA FoodKeeper guidance for a refrigerator at 4 °C / 40 °F or below. They are a reminder aid, not food-safety advice.

Appendix: cover photo options (not part of the article)

Five ideas to shoot at home with a phone, no AI-generated imagery. The sketches only show the composition. Before shooting, check the cover size Builder Center asks for when you upload. Shoot landscape and leave empty space on the side marked for the title, so the image survives cropping.

General tips: natural light from a window, phone on a stack of books or a tripod, the Telegram chat in light mode with a real alert on screen, and no faces or personal names visible unless you want them.

A. The mystery container story

  • Open fridge at night, lit only by the fridge light.
  • An old container with masking tape that reads "¿?", half pulled out.
  • A hand in the foreground holds a phone showing the bot's question with the three buttons.
  • Leave the dark left side for the title.

B. Flat lay after the supermarket features

  • Shot from above on a wooden table or counter.
  • Two or three containers with handwritten tape labels, a can, a grocery receipt, and the phone with the shopping-list confirmation on screen.
  • Leave the right third empty (a plain tablecloth works) for the title.

C. Two generations, one fridge impact

  • Hands only: an older hand and a younger hand, each holding a phone with the same alert ("Mamá has expired soup to review").
  • Fridge door blurred in the background.
  • Tells the shared-fridge story without showing faces.

D. The fridge door is the state machine recommended

  • Sticky notes on the fridge door laid out like the workflow: Stored → Wait → Reminder → Ask? → Eaten / Review, with marker arrows.
  • A phone held next to it, or stuck with a magnet, showing the question with buttons.
  • Speaks straight to a Step Functions audience and is easy to recreate.

E. The printed date detail

  • Close-up of a can lid or a package showing its printed expiry date, with a phone camera framing it.
  • Shows the newest feature (pantry food by photo).
  • Works best as a second image inside the article rather than as the cover.
